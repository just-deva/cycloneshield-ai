PART A - THE PLAIN-LANGUAGE EXPLANATION
##############################################################################################


A1. THE PROBLEM
---------------

A cyclone is like a very large, slow, spinning machine that we can see coming for days. India's
weather service (IMD) is good at telling us where the machine is now, where it will probably go,
and how strong it will be. That warning system is one of the best in the world, and it works: in
1999 the Odisha "Super Cyclone" killed roughly 10,000 people; in 2013 (Phailin) and 2019 (Fani),
storms of similar or greater power killed a few dozen, because people were warned early and about
a million were moved to safety each time.

But there is a gap between "a storm is coming" and "here is exactly what to do in my district".

Analogy: Imagine a weather app tells you "heavy rain tomorrow". That is useful. But what you
actually need to know is: "Will MY street flood? At what time? Is the road to the hospital still
open? By when must I leave, and which way do I go?" A general forecast cannot answer those
questions. A District Collector (the senior officer of a district) has exactly those questions,
multiplied by hundreds of hospitals, power stations, roads and shelters, three days before the
storm arrives:

   - Which hospitals will lose power or be cut off, and when?
   - Which electrical substations will be flooded or hit by destructive wind?
   - Which roads will be under water, so which evacuation routes still work?
   - Which shelters are safe, and by what time must people leave to reach them?
   - Who must be told what, in which language, and who approves the message?
   - Should relief money be released now, before the storm, instead of after?

Today much of this is done by hand: officers lay forecast maps over lists of buildings, phone
departments, and write messages under time pressure. Acting BEFORE the storm (evacuating,
protecting equipment, releasing pre-agreed funds) saves lives and livelihoods. Acting AFTER is
recovery. Our project is about closing that last step between "there is a forecast" and
"everyone who must act knows exactly what to do".


A2. WHAT WE BUILT, IN ONE PARAGRAPH
-----------------------------------

CycloneShield AI is like a "navigation app for a cyclone", built for the officers who must
respond. It does NOT predict the cyclone - IMD does that, and we trust IMD. It takes the official
forecast and answers: "So what, for MY district, hour by hour?" It works out what the storm will
do to the ground (wind, sea water, rain), checks that against real hospitals, power stations,
roads and shelters, plans safe evacuation routes with "leave before" times, drafts the warning
messages in local languages, and - only after human officers approve - sends them out and keeps a
tamper-evident record of everything.


A3. THE BIG IDEA: "NUMBERS FROM CODE, WORDS FROM THE AI"
--------------------------------------------------------

The single most important design decision in the project is this:

   Every NUMBER (wind speed, flood depth, time of arrival) is calculated by ordinary, checkable
   computer code. The AI (Google's Gemini) is only allowed to READ documents and to WRITE and
   EXPLAIN in words. And even then, a separate piece of code checks every number in what the AI
   wrote against the calculated numbers before any human sees it.

Analogy: Think of a newspaper. The AI is a good reporter who writes clearly and can translate
into many languages. But the reporter is not allowed to make up statistics: a fact-checker sits
next to them with the original spreadsheet and a red pen. If the reporter writes a number that is
not in the spreadsheet, the article is sent back. And nothing is published until an editor - a
human - signs it off. In our system the "spreadsheet" is called the FACTS PACKET, the
"fact-checker" is called the VALIDATOR, and the "editor" is the approving officer.

Why this matters: AI language models can sound confident while being wrong ("hallucination"). In
a cyclone, a wrong number can mean someone is told to stay in a place that floods. So we never
let the AI be the source of a number, and we never let it send anything on its own.


A4. THE PARTS OF THE SYSTEM, EACH WITH AN ANALOGY
--------------------------------------------------

Step 1 - READING THE STORM
   What it does: Takes the official storm track. Either a replay of a real past cyclone, so
   anyone can try the tool today, or an official IMD bulletin (a PDF or a picture), which Gemini
   reads and turns into a structured table of positions and strengths.
   Analogy: A translator who reads a letter in a hard-to-parse format and copies the key facts
   onto a clean form. A supervisor (our validation code) then checks the form: "A wind of 900 km/h?
   A position in the middle of Africa? Times out of order?" - and flags anything impossible.

Step 2 - WIND
   What it does: Estimates how strong the wind is at every point around the storm.
   Analogy: Ripples spreading from a stone dropped in a pond - but spinning. Right at the "eye" it
   is calm; just outside it the wind is the fiercest (the "eyewall"); it fades with distance.
   We use a well-known published formula (Holland, 1980) that draws this shape. The side of the
   storm where the storm's own spin adds to its forward movement is stronger, so the wind is
   stronger to the right of the track (in the Northern Hemisphere) than to the left.

Step 3 - STORM SURGE (SEA WATER PUSHED ONTO LAND)
   What it does: Estimates how high the sea will rise at the coast, for each stretch of coast.
   Analogy: Two things push the sea up. (1) Low air pressure in the storm acts like a lightly
   lifted plunger: the sea surface bulges up under it, about 1 cm for each hectopascal of
   pressure drop. (2) Strong wind blowing toward the shore pushes water ahead of it, like blowing
   across a plate of soup so it piles up at the far edge. A shallow, wide sea shelf makes that
   piling-up worse, like soup in a wide, shallow plate. We add the normal tide on top.

Step 4 - FLOODING ON LAND
   What it does: Decides which land floods, using a map of ground heights.
   Analogy: A bathtub with a twist. Imagine slowly filling the map with water up to a certain
   level: everything lower than the water level floods. The twist is that we only flood places
   that are actually connected to the sea, like a bathtub where the water only reaches the parts
   joined to the tap - a low field behind a hill does not flood just because it is low. And the
   water level fades as it travels inland (0.2 metres lower for every kilometre), because the
   surge loses strength as it spreads, like a wave losing height as it runs up a beach.

Step 5 - RAIN
   What it does: Estimates how much rain falls and where it will collect or rush.
   Analogy: Pour a bucket of water over a crumpled bedsheet. The water collects in the dips
   ("waterlogging basins", puddles) and runs along the folds ("runoff corridors", small streams).
   We use a standard formula for how much rain a cyclone brings, and a terrain algorithm to find
   the dips and the folds.

Step 6 - "WHAT IF?" (THE NINE SCENARIOS)
   What it does: A forecast can be a little wrong. So we re-run everything nine times: the storm
   nudged 40 km to one side, on the official track, or 40 km to the other; and weaker, as
   forecast, or stronger by 10 knots. For each building we report "hit in 7 of 9 scenarios".
   Analogy: Planning to catch a bus that might be five minutes early or late. You do not plan for
   one exact minute; you plan for the range, and you notice which choices are safe in all cases.
   Important: "7 of 9" is a spread of what-ifs, not a mathematically calibrated probability, and
   the tool says so.

Step 7 - CHECKING REAL BUILDINGS AND ROADS
   What it does: Takes real hospitals, power substations, power lines, main roads and possible
   shelters (from the open map OpenStreetMap) and checks each against the calculated wind, water
   and rain, using plain, published thresholds. The result is a "what gets hit, and when" table.
   Analogy: A building inspector's checklist with a traffic-light result: OK, Watch, At risk,
   Critical - always with the reasons written next to it ("wind above 118 km/h at 03:00", "flood
   deeper than 1 metre at the hospital site"). No mystery scoring.

Step 8 - EVACUATION ROUTES
   What it does: For each locality, finds a route to a safe shelter that avoids flooded roads,
   and says the latest time you must leave. If there is no safe route, it says so plainly:
   "shelter in place / request assistance" instead of leaving a blank.
   Analogy: A GPS that knows which roads are underwater, plus an alarm clock: "leave before 14:30".

Step 9 - HOW SERIOUS IS IT? (THE WARNING TIER)
   What it does: Decides the alert level - Monitor, Yellow, Orange, Red - using written rules,
   not the AI's opinion. It looks at two clocks and takes the lower one: (1) how far along
   IMD's own official four-stage warning system we are (Watch 72 hours out, Alert 48, Warning 24,
   Post-landfall 12), and (2) how bad our calculated hazard actually is. If there is no real
   hazard for the district, the tier stays at "Monitor - no action", however close the clock is.
   Analogy: A traffic light controlled by a rule book, not a mood. A storm that misses the
   district does not raise a false alarm (we test this with a near-miss storm, Montha 2025).

Step 10 - WRITING THE MESSAGES
   What it does: Drafts a separate advisory for each audience - collector, hospital, power
   company, roads department, shelter managers, fishermen - in the local language (Telugu, Odia,
   Hindi, Bengali, Tamil, Vietnamese...). Gemini writes; the validator checks: every number came
   from the facts packet, every place name is real, there are no web links or phone numbers, no
   hidden "ignore your instructions" tricks, and the message admits uncertainty. If it fails, the AI
   gets one more try with the validator's feedback; if it still fails, we use a plain fill-in-the-
   blanks template, clearly labelled as such.
   Analogy: A speechwriter and a fact-checker, as described in A3.

Step 11 - APPROVAL AND SENDING
   What it does: A message is only sent after a human approves it. For serious messages (Orange
   and Red) TWO different officers must approve. Then it is sent (Telegram with an
   "Acknowledge" button, a standard machine-readable alert file called CAP, and a webhook), and
   every action is written to a log.
   Analogy: The "two-key" rule for a bank vault: one person prepares, a different person
   approves, and for the most serious actions two approvers are needed. The AI has no key.

Step 12 - THE UNFORGEABLE LOGBOOK (AUDIT LOG)
   What it does: Records every action - who drafted, who approved, what was sent, who
   acknowledged. Each entry contains a fingerprint of the previous entry.
   Analogy: A ledger where each page is stamped with a fingerprint of the page before it. If
   someone secretly changes or tears out an old page, the fingerprints on every later page no
   longer match, so the tampering shows. (We honestly call it "tamper-evident", not "tamper-
   proof", and we do not call it a blockchain.)

Step 13 - RELEASING MONEY EARLY (ANTICIPATORY FINANCE)
   What it does: Governments and aid agencies can agree in advance that "if the forecast crosses
   this line, release X% of a relief fund now". We show which line the current forecast crosses,
   draft a memo, and list the risks that the rule fires when it shouldn't or misses a real loss
   ("basis risk"). No money moves; it is a recommendation for a human to approve.
   Analogy: Travel insurance that pays out when the airline announces a cancellation, rather
   than after you have filed a receipt.

Step 14 - THE TRUST PANEL
   What it does: A screen that shows how every number was made, the assumptions, how well the
   model matches past events (and where it is only a fit, not a proof), every AI call that was
   made, and a "verify the logbook" button.
   Analogy: A car's dashboard and service history - you can look under the bonnet.

Extra - SPARE TYRES (WHEN THINGS FAIL)
   Every outside service can fail: the AI service may be busy or out of quota, the satellite-data
   service may be unavailable, the phone messaging may be down. The tool never crashes; each has a
   labelled fallback (template message; open terrain data; simulated sending). Real AI answers are
   also saved (a "cache") so a demo can still show real output; such answers are marked "(cached)"
   and re-checked against the current numbers every time. Nothing is ever faked.

Extra - MAKING IT WORK ELSEWHERE (TENANTS)
   A "tenant" is one region's settings in one small file: where it is, which languages, which
   storms to replay. Adding another Indian state - or another country - means adding a file and
   running two data scripts, not rewriting the software. We ship three: Andhra Pradesh
   (Visakhapatnam), Odisha (Puri) and Vietnam (Hai Phong).
   Analogy: A franchise. The recipe (software) is the same everywhere; each branch has its own
   local menu (settings).


A5. A WALK THROUGH ONE SCENARIO
-------------------------------

"What if Cyclone Hudhud, which struck Visakhapatnam in October 2014, came today - with today's
hospitals, substations and roads?"

   1. An officer opens the app: region Andhra Pradesh, storm Hudhud 2014. The map shows the storm
      track and hundreds of real hospitals, substations and shelters.
   2. The officer drags the clock to "24 hours before landfall". The alert level turns ORANGE by
      rule, and assets light up as the storm reaches them.
   3. The "Assets" table lists every hospital and substation, its status, what will hit it (wind,
      sea water, rain), when it is first hit, and "hit in 9 of 9 scenarios".
   4. "Roads and evacuation" shows which main roads are waterlogged, which localities have a safe
      route to a shelter (24 of 30 in this run), the latest departure times, and which places are
      stranded.
   5. In "Advisories" the officer chooses hospital + Telugu and presses Draft. Gemini writes it;
      green ticks appear when the checks pass.
   6. Two different officers approve. The message is sent to a phone through Telegram with an
      "Acknowledge" button; pressing it marks the message acknowledged in the log.
   7. "Finance" shows which early-release tier the forecast reaches, with a draft memo.
   8. Switching to Montha 2025 (a storm that missed the coast) keeps the level at "Monitor - no
      action": the system does not cry wolf.
   9. Switching the region to Odisha or Vietnam runs the same pipeline with no code changes.


A6. WHAT IS REAL AND WHAT IS SIMULATED
--------------------------------------

Being honest about this is part of the design.

   REAL     : real historical storm tracks; real OpenStreetMap hospitals/substations/roads; real
              terrain data; Gemini calls (when the key has quota); Telegram messages with a
              working Acknowledge button; webhook delivery; the approval workflow; the
              tamper-evident log; live satellite/weather data through Google Earth Engine.
   SIMULATED: SMS, WhatsApp, Cell Broadcast and publishing into India's national alert system
              (SACHET) - these are controlled by telecom operators and the government, so we do
              not send them; the tool shows what would be sent and labels it as simulated. All the
              alert files we produce are marked "Exercise" and "Restricted" so they can never be
              mistaken for a real public alert.
   ASSUMED  : storm-surge results are "screening level" (good for ranking risk, not a substitute
              for the official models); the finance percentages are illustrative.


A7. WHO BENEFITS AND WHY IT MATTERS
-----------------------------------

   - District officers get, in minutes, the asset-by-asset picture they now assemble by hand.
   - Hospitals, power utilities and road departments get an advisory written for THEM, with times.
   - Communities benefit from earlier, clearer instructions in their own language and from
     evacuation routes that account for flooded roads.
   - Funders and planners get a transparent, pre-agreed link between forecast and early funds.
   - Other states and countries can adopt it by adding a configuration file, so the same tool can
     scale across Indian states and to BRICS and other cyclone-affected partners.

In short: IMD tells us the storm is coming. CycloneShield AI helps the district decide what to do,
and when - fast, checkably, and with a human always in charge.

B0. SYSTEM AT A GLANCE
----------------------

Pipeline (left to right):

  IMD bulletin (PDF/PNG/text) --Gemini multimodal--> validated Storm
  Replay library (JTWC best tracks) ------------------> Storm
                                   Storm --> Hazard engine (wind, surge, inundation, rain,
                                              pluvial pathways, 9-scenario ensemble)
  OSM exposure per region + terrain ------> Asset impact + evacuation routing
                                   Impact --> FACTS PACKET (sole source of numbers)
                                   Facts  --> Gemini structured draft --> deterministic VALIDATOR
                                   Validated draft --> maker-checker approval --> dispatch
                                              (CAP 1.2, Telegram + acknowledge, webhook)
                                   Every step --> hash-chained audit log
                                   Impact --> Officer copilot (Gemini function calling)
                                   Impact --> Anticipatory-finance trigger monitor

Stack: Python 3.11, FastAPI, NumPy, SciPy, NetworkX, Pillow, httpx; google-genai (Gemini API);
earthengine-api (Google Earth Engine); a static single-page UI (Leaflet + marker clusters, vanilla
ES modules, no build step) served by the same FastAPI process; Docker; Cloud Run. About 4,500
lines of backend Python and 55 automated tests.

Five design principles (they explain most decisions below):
  1. Numbers come from code; words come from the model.
  2. Every AI output is checked before a person can act on it, and the checks are visible.
  3. A human approves anything that is sent; the model has no dispatch capability.
  4. Fail soft and say so: every dependency has a labelled degraded mode.
  5. Configuration over code: regions, languages, thresholds and shelf geometry live in data files.

B2. DATA INPUTS AND PROVENANCE
------------------------------

  Storm tracks      JTWC best-track archive via the UCAR RAL open mirror. Eight storms: Hudhud 2014,
                    Phailin 2013, Fani 2019, Amphan 2020, Michaung 2023, Montha 2025, ONE-26
                    (Sep 2026), Yagi 2024.
  Infrastructure    OpenStreetMap extracts (ODbL) prepared OFFLINE by scripts/fetch_osm.py - the
                    running app never calls Overpass. Hospitals, substations, power lines, arterial
                    roads and bridges, candidate shelters, localities.
  Terrain           AWS/Mapzen Terrarium tiles (SRTM-derived, about 36 m), cached as arrays.
  Earth Engine      NOAA/GFS0P25 (forecast rain), JRC/GHSL/P2023A/GHS_POP (population),
                    GOOGLE/Research/open-buildings/v3/polygons (buildings), MERIT/Hydro/v1_0_1
                    (height above nearest drainage). Deprecated asset IDs were deliberately avoided
                    (COPERNICUS/DEM/GLO30, FAO/GAUL/2015/level2, SMAP SPL4SMGP/007).
  Official guidance IMD bulletins, read by Gemini (see B7.4).

Decision: replay storms from best tracks rather than a "live" feed. Reason: live cyclone feeds need
authorised access and cannot be demonstrated on demand; replays run any time, let judges reproduce
results, and share the exact pipeline that would consume an official forecast. The cost is
hindsight: a best track is an after-the-fact analysis, not a forecast, and the ensemble only partly
represents real forecast error. This is disclosed in the UI and in the deck.


B3. STORM INPUT (app/storms/)
-----------------------------

B3.1 ATCF parser (atcf.py). Parses the ATCF b-deck format: latitude/longitude in tenths of a
     degree, wind in knots, MSLP, 34/50/64-kt wind radii and radius of maximum wind (RMW); also
     the JTWC .tcw forecast format.
B3.2 Averaging periods. JTWC winds are 1-minute sustained, IMD winds are 3-minute; the basis is
     stored per storm and never silently mixed (1-minute values run a few percent higher).
B3.3 Track utilities (track.py). Time interpolation; closest approach to the region's focus point,
     which defines T0 for the "event clock"; what-if scenarios that shift the track cross-track
     by a distance or scale intensity, scaling pressure consistently (Atkinson-Holland relation).
B3.4 Why an event clock relative to closest approach: it makes advisories comparable across storms
     and lines the tool up with IMD's lead-time-based stages.


B4. THE HAZARD ENGINE (app/hazard/, app/engine.py)
--------------------------------------------------

B4.1 Wind (wind.py) - Holland (1980) parametric profile
     p(r)  = pc + dp * exp(-(Rm/r)^B)
     Vg(r) = sqrt( (B*dp/rho) * (Rm/r)^B * exp(-(Rm/r)^B) + (r*f/2)^2 ) - r*f/2
     B     = rho * e * Vg_max^2 / dp, clamped to [1.0, 2.5]
     Surface wind = 0.85 * Vg (surface reduction factor), rotated 20 degrees inward (inflow),
     plus 0.5 * the storm's translation vector (right-of-track asymmetry).
     Rm comes from the best track where present, else a default by intensity (25-60 km).
     Why Holland: it is the de-facto standard parametric profile, needs only a handful of
     parameters that a bulletin provides, and is fast enough to evaluate on a grid for nine
     scenarios interactively. Alternatives (full NWP or a hydrodynamic vortex model) need data and
     compute we do not have and could not explain to a Collector. The 0.85 / 20 deg / 0.5 constants
     are standard-practice choices and are listed as assumptions in the app. A unit test checks
     that the peak matches the reported maximum wind within 5 % and lies at the RMW.

B4.2 Storm surge (surge.py) - screening-level storm-tide budget, per coastal sector
     storm tide = k * ( inverse barometer + wind set-up ) + tide      (tide: a configured assumption,
                                                                      default 0.4 m, adjustable in the UI)
     inverse barometer = 1 cm per hPa of pressure drop at the sector (0.01 m/hPa)
     wind set-up       = tau * L / (rho_w * g * h)
     tau               = rho_a * Cd * U_onshore^2
     Cd                = min( (0.75 + 0.067 U) * 1e-3 , 2.5e-3 )     (Cd saturates at 2.5e-3)
     L, h              = effective shelf width and mean depth of the sector (hand-set, in tenant
                         config); U_onshore = wind component pointing inland, obtained from the
                         gradient of distance-to-sea on the DEM. Only the onshore side of the
                         track contributes, so surge is asymmetric as in reality.
     k                 = per-coast calibration multiplier (see B12).
     Why a budget, not a hydrodynamic model: ADCIRC/SLOSH-class models need bathymetry, meshes and
     hours of compute, and INCOIS/IMD already run the operational versions. A steady-state budget of
     the same class as forecasters' nomograms is enough to rank sectors and drive asset thresholds,
     and every term can be shown to a non-specialist. CLIMADA's TCSurgeBathtub (Xu 2010) is
     computed only as a cross-check reference.

B4.3 Flooding (inundation.py, dem.py) - sea-connected, distance-attenuated bathtub
     A cell floods when   elevation < storm_tide - 0.2 m/km * distance_from_sea
     AND it is hydraulically connected to the sea through other flooded cells (connected-component
     labelling). A plain bathtub would flood inland depressions the sea cannot reach; connectivity
     removes those false positives. Water bodies at or below 0 m are excluded; harbours whose
     entrance is narrower than one cell are merged into the sea. Depth is reported in CLASSES
     (< 0.5, 0.5-1.5, > 1.5 m) because DEM vertical error (about metres) dominates surge error, so
     centimetre depths would be false precision.
     Known bias: the DEM is a surface model, biased high under buildings and trees, so flooding in
     built-up areas is UNDER-predicted. Stated in the UI and the deck.

B4.4 Rain and pathways (rain.py, pluvial.py)
     R-CLIPER (Tuleya, DeMaria & Kuligowski 2007): a track-only, radially symmetric rain-rate
     climatology. The 24-hour maximum is compared with IMD classes: heavy 64.5, very heavy 115.6,
     extremely heavy 204.5 mm.
     Terrain: priority-flood depression filling (Barnes, Lehman & Mulla 2014) finds waterlogging
     basins (closed depressions 0.5-3 m deep; stricter thresholds in flat deltas where DEM noise
     exceeds shallow depressions); D8 flow accumulation finds runoff corridors (>= 0.5 km2
     upstream). A "damage pathway" = source (rain) -> water path -> receptor (an asset) -> onset
     time.
     Limitation: R-CLIPER has no orography and under-predicts stalled or monsoon-interacting
     storms (e.g. Michaung over Chennai). It is a scenario prior, not a forecast, so the tool
     also reads live NOAA GFS rain from Earth Engine as context (B10).

B4.5 Ensemble (engine.py)
     Nine members: cross-track offset {-40, 0, +40} km x intensity offset {-10, 0, +10} kt. For
     each asset: "k of 9", the share of scenarios in which it reaches a status. Surge is reported
     as a range across members. This is a SPREAD, not a calibrated probability, and is labelled.
     Why 9: a 3 x 3 grid covers the dominant error sources (track and intensity) at nine times
     the base cost, which the engine can afford interactively; it is a design choice, documented.

B4.6 Performance and caching
     Simulation results are cached in an in-memory LRU (24 entries) keyed by (tenant, storm,
     parameters); layers (inundation and pathways PNGs) are served from those results. A warm-up
     thread pre-computes each tenant's default replay after startup so the first click is instant.
     One uvicorn worker with threads, because the engine is CPU-bound and holds per-tenant arrays
     in memory.


B5. EXPOSURE AND IMPACT (app/exposure/)
---------------------------------------

B5.1 Assets. Hospitals, substations, lines, arterial roads/bridges, candidate shelters (schools
     and halls unless designated), and localities (evacuation origins). Ways are clipped to the
     study area. OSM coverage is uneven: good for high-voltage lines, main roads and urban
     hospitals; poor for distribution poles and designated cyclone shelters. Schools and halls are
     therefore shown as CANDIDATE shelters and capacity is not modelled.

B5.2 Impact rules (impact.py) - explicit, cited thresholds, no learned model
     Wind bands aligned with IMD classes: gale 62, damaging 89, destructive 118, extreme 167 km/h.
     Surge depth: 0.3 m makes a road impassable (vehicle-passability threshold after Pregnolato
     et al. 2017); 0.5 m critical for a substation; 1.0 m critical for a hospital. Rain: IMD
     heavy/very-heavy over a waterlogging basin -> possible/likely waterlogging.
     Status is one of ok / watch / at risk / critical, with the reasons and the time of first
     impact. Shelters are made unusable only by flooding (they exist to ride out wind); non-
     designated candidates are flagged at extreme wind.
     Why rules and not machine learning: no labelled outcome data exists for these assets; rules
     are auditable by officers and defensible; every threshold is visible in the Trust panel.

B5.3 Expected loss ratio (used by finance). Emanuel (2011) sigmoid f = v^3 / (1 + v^3), with
     v = max(V - V_thresh, 0) / (V_half - V_thresh), V_thresh = 25.7 m/s, V_half = 58.7 m/s for the
     North Indian Ocean (calibration shipped with CLIMADA; Eberenz, Luthi & Bresch 2021). It is an
     AGGREGATE expected fraction of exposed value - near zero below about 50 kt - not a structural
     failure probability for any one building.

B5.4 Evacuation routing (routing.py)
     NetworkX on the OSM road graph; edge weights from class-based free-flow speeds. Nodes are
     blocked at flood depth >= 0.3 m or inside waterlogging basins under very heavy rain. A multi-
     source Dijkstra from all SAFE shelters gives each locality its nearest reachable shelter.
     "Leave before" = local gale onset - travel time - 1 h buffer. A locality with no route
     gets an explicit "shelter in place / request assistance" verdict rather than a blank.
     Why multi-source from shelters: one search serves all localities (fast) and yields the nearest
     shelter by travel time under the flood-aware graph.


B6. THE WARNING TIER: RULES, NOT THE LLM (ai/facts.py: severity())
------------------------------------------------------------------

     tier = min( IMD stage reached at this lead time , modelled hazard level )
     IMD stages: Pre-Cyclone Watch (72 h), Cyclone Alert (48 h), Cyclone Warning (24 h),
     Post-Landfall Outlook (12 h). Output: Monitor / Yellow / Orange / Red.

     Why min(): the stage clock alone would alert for a storm that misses the district; the
     modelled hazard alone would ignore the institutional warning process. Taking the lower means
     neither can escalate on its own. The tier sets advisory wording, the number of approvers
     (two for orange/red) and the finance tier. The LLM never sets severity - a deliberate safety
     decision, verified by tests and shown on the near-miss replay (Montha 2025: "Monitor").


B7. THE AI LAYER (app/ai/)
--------------------------

B7.1 gemini_client.py - one wrapper for every model call
     - Model chain: gemini-3.7-flash (the model the track names), then 3.6, then 3.5-flash-lite;
       the primary ID is configurable (GEMINI_MODEL, GEMINI_FALLBACK_MODELS).
     - thinking_level set explicitly: Gemini 3 defaults to high (slow and costly for our short
       structured tasks); MINIMAL is unsupported on 3.7 Flash (HTTP 400), so it is served as LOW.
     - Temperature left at default (Gemini 3 guidance; lowering it can degrade reasoning).
     - Structured output via response_schema for drafts and bulletins; automatic function calling
       for the copilot.
     - Resilience: retries with a CIRCUIT BREAKER - a model that returns 429/402/503 is skipped for
       a period rather than hammered - and a 75 s overall budget, so a slow model cannot hang the
       UI. A trace record (model, latency, tool calls, fallback used) feeds the UI's AI-trace panel.
     Why one wrapper: retries, tracing, budgets and fallbacks live in one place instead of being
     re-implemented per feature.

B7.2 facts.py - the FACTS PACKET
     A JSON object of computed numbers, with an audience-specific view (collector, hospital,
     power, roads, shelter, fisheries). It is the only source of numbers an advisory may contain.
     Design choice: rather than trying to make the model "not hallucinate", we remove the need to
     trust it - anything not in the packet is, by construction, invalid.

B7.3 advisory.py - drafting pipeline
     facts packet -> Gemini structured draft -> validator -> (optional) translation, validated
     again -> officer review/approval. If validation fails, ONE retry with the validator's
     feedback; if it still fails, a deterministic template, labelled "template". Officers may edit;
     edits are re-validated and clear existing approvals.

B7.4 bulletin.py - multimodal ingestion
     IMD publishes cyclone bulletins as PDF/PNG only. Gemini extracts a typed schema (Bulletin).
     Deterministic code then normalises it: km/h -> kt; drops implausible positions (outside
     0-35 N, 40-110 E) or winds (outside 10-200 kt); orders times; reports every issue. A surge
     sentence in the bulletin is carried through as OFFICIAL GUIDANCE and shown beside our own
     estimate. The UI shows extracted-vs-source so an officer can verify the reading.

B7.5 validator.py - deterministic guardrails (every draft AND every translation)
     (1) Every number appears in the facts packet, is a rounded variant of one, or is a documented
         threshold. Digits in any script (Telugu, Odia, Devanagari, ...) are normalised first so a
         translation cannot smuggle in a changed number.
     (2) Every named place or asset exists in the model output.
     (3) No URLs, phone numbers, or instruction-like text ("ignore previous instructions").
     (4) An uncertainty statement is present.
     (5) The schema is complete.
     Rationale: a response schema guarantees syntax, not truth; Google's own guidance says to
     validate values in the application. Untrusted text (bulletins, feeds, OSM names) is treated
     as data, never as instructions.

B7.6 copilot.py - officer Q&A
     Gemini with function calling over seven tools: get_overview, list_assets_at_risk,
     list_road_impacts, evacuation_status, sector_surge, explain_method, draft_advisory_preview.
     Tools never raise into the model (a wrapper returns an error object); answers are number-
     checked against tool outputs; there is NO dispatch tool. If the model returns an empty
     answer, the copilot re-asks with the tool results included.

B7.7 aicache.py - persistent cache of REAL Gemini outputs
     Problem: model quota, credits or availability can fail exactly when a prototype is being
     evaluated. Solution: every real output is stored keyed by the SHA-256 of the exact facts
     packet it was written from. For identical facts it may be served again, RE-VALIDATED against
     the current facts, and labelled "(cached)" with model and date; it can always be regenerated
     live. A tampered cache file is rejected by the validator. Nothing is fabricated: with no cache
     and no working model, the labelled template is used. scripts/prime_ai_cache.py fills the cache.

B7.8 trace.py - ring buffer behind the "AI trace" panel, so every call, fallback and cache hit is
     visible to a reviewer.


B8. DISPATCH (app/dispatch/)
----------------------------

B8.1 Workflow (workflow.py). State machine DRAFT -> PENDING_APPROVAL -> APPROVED -> DISPATCHED ->
     ACKED, with REJECTED and CANCELLED. Maker-checker: the approver must differ from the maker;
     orange and red need two distinct approvers. Illegal transitions are refused server-side, so
     the UI cannot bypass them.

B8.2 CAP 1.2 (cap.py). Common Alerting Protocol XML in the profile India's SACHET uses (cap:
     namespace, "sent" with a +05:30 offset, geocode valueName "LGD District Code" when
     configured). Always status=Exercise and scope=Restricted with our own sender id; the
     disclaimer is in <note>; includes a recommended area polygon, an optional second <info> in
     the officer's language, and Cancel messages that reference the original. A structural safety
     check runs before every dispatch. Why: so an authorised originator could ingest our output,
     while making it impossible to mistake it for a public alert or to impersonate IMD/SDMA.

B8.3 Channels (channels.py). REAL: Telegram (message + CAP file + inline Acknowledge button; a
     press marks the advisory acknowledged and is audit-logged; long-polling for local
     development, webhook on Cloud Run) and an outbound webhook POST (default: the app's own
     mock DEOC receiver). SIMULATED AND LABELLED: SMS (TRAI DLT gateways), WhatsApp Business and
     Cell Broadcast, which are telecom/government gated. If a Telegram acknowledgement update
     cannot be processed, the failure is logged, an audit entry "telegram_ack_failed" is written and
     the button press is answered so the officer knows.

B8.4 Audit log (audit.py). Append-only JSON lines; entry_n contains hash(entry_(n-1) || payload).
     verify() recomputes the chain and reports the first broken index. Tamper-EVIDENT against
     non-re-chained edits; a determined attacker with write access could recompute all later
     hashes, so production should anchor the chain head in a retention-locked bucket and sign
     entries with Cloud KMS (roadmap). We deliberately do not call it a blockchain or
     "tamper-proof".


B9. ANTICIPATORY FINANCE (app/finance.py)
-----------------------------------------

     Design references: CCRIF (parametric payout when MODELLED loss reaches an attachment point) and
     the IFRC / Bangladesh Red Crescent cyclone Early Action Protocol (pre-agreed funds released on a
     wind forecast above a threshold at a set lead time).
     Four cumulative tiers keyed to IMD's stage clock: T0 Readiness 10 %, T1 Alert 35 %, T2
     Warning 75 %, T3 Landfall 100 % of a pre-arranged pool, each with its hazard condition. The
     monitor shows which conditions are met, drafts a release-recommendation memo, lists BASIS
     RISK (a modelled trigger can fire without loss or miss real loss), and requires a human
     approval. No money moves. The percentages and the pool are ILLUSTRATIVE and labelled so.


B10. GOOGLE EARTH ENGINE (app/hazard/gee.py)
--------------------------------------------

     Adds real-time and satellite-derived context to the open-terrain engine:
       - live_rain: latest NOAA GFS 0.25-degree run, summing 6-hourly precipitation buckets to give
         24 h and 72 h totals at the focus point and the maximum in the study area. Coarse (does not
         resolve the cyclone core): context, not a track-aware forecast.
       - flood_exposure: population (GHSL P2023A, 100 m) and buildings (Open Buildings v3,
         confidence >= 0.7) inside the MODELLED flood zone (250 m squares, >= 25 % wet). Read as
         order-of-magnitude exposure, not a census. (Hudhud replay: about 1,327 people and 318
         buildings.)
       - tile_layers: map tiles for GFS rain, MERIT Hydro HAND (< 10 m) and population density.
     Engineering decisions:
       - Authentication via Application Default Credentials (Cloud Run service account) or
         "earthengine authenticate" locally; the Cloud project must be registered for Earth Engine
         (non-commercial registration used here).
       - FAILS SOFT: status() explains why EE is unavailable; the rest of the product keeps working.
       - Non-blocking start: initialisation runs in a background thread; NO_GCE_CHECK avoids a 24 s
         metadata-server probe off Cloud Run.
       - Speed: getMapId takes tens of seconds per layer, so the three tile layers are fetched in
         parallel and cached (globally, since layers are region-independent); a warm-up thread
         pre-computes rain and tiles after init.
     Constraint stated openly: Earth Engine is free for non-commercial use only; a state pilot
     would likely need a paid plan.


B11. BACKEND API, STATE AND FRONT END
-------------------------------------

B11.1 API. FastAPI with OpenAPI docs at /docs. Groups: status (health, gee/status, ai-cache,
      trace); regions and storms; simulation and layers; trust (methodology, calibration, audit,
      audit/verify); advisories (draft, edit, submit, approve, reject, dispatch, ack, cancel,
      cap.xml); AI (copilot, bulletin extract); finance; Earth Engine (summary, tiles); Telegram
      (webhook, setup, status); mock DEOC. Input is validated with Pydantic (e.g. cross_track_km
      within +-150, delta_kt -40..+60).
B11.2 State. Runtime state (advisories.json, and the audit.jsonl chain) is written to files under
      CYCLONESHIELD_STATE_DIR. That is adequate for a prototype with one instance; production
      would use Firestore and a retention-locked bucket. Hence Cloud Run --max-instances 1.
B11.3 Front end. A single-page UI with no build step: Leaflet map with marker clusters; tabs
      Overview, Assets, Roads & evac, Advisories, Copilot, Finance, Trust; an event clock with
      T-72/T-48/T-24/T-12 markers; a layer toggle panel; a what-if panel. All inserted text is
      HTML-escaped. A no-cache (ETag revalidate) middleware ensures a redeploy is never masked by a
      stale script - added after a stale-cache bug during development.
      Why no framework/build: fewer moving parts, one container, instant start, and it is easy for
      reviewers to read.


B12. VALIDATION, CALIBRATION AND HONEST LIMITS
----------------------------------------------

B12.1 Automated tests (55). Cover: parsers (ATCF, .tcw); physics benchmarks (Holland peak vs
      reported max within 5 % and at the RMW; surge worked examples); API contracts; the validator
      (rejects invented numbers, injected text, unknown place names); approval rules (maker !=
      checker; two approvers for orange/red); CAP safety fields; audit tamper detection; finance
      tiers; bulletin normalisation; the AI cache including tamper rejection; the Telegram
      acknowledgement flow.
B12.2 Surge calibration (READ CAREFULLY). Hand-set shelf geometry plus ONE multiplier k per coast,
      fitted on a single observed event:
        Andhra Pradesh  - Hudhud 2014, Visakhapatnam tide gauge 1.2-1.4 m  -> k = 2.0
                          modelled port 1.27 m (nine-scenario range 0.41-1.46 m)
        Odisha          - Fani 2019, about 1.5 m reported at Puri (INCOIS guidance had been up to
                          about 4 m)                                          -> k = 0.65
                          modelled Puri beach 1.53 m (range 0.13-1.71 m)
        Vietnam         - UNCALIBRATED (k = 1.0)
      Because k was fitted to these observations, agreement is BY CONSTRUCTION and is NOT a
      validation. That the two Indian coasts need such different k (2.0 vs 0.65) shows the hand-set
      shelf width/depth is the weakest part of the surge model. The UI's Trust > Calibration tab
      states this, and surge is never presented as an official forecast. First item of future work:
      derive shelf parameters from bathymetry and validate out-of-sample against Sentinel-1 flood
      extents.
B12.3 Other limitations, all disclosed in the product: DEM surface bias (built-up flooding
      under-predicted); R-CLIPER has no orography; OSM gaps (distribution poles, designated
      shelters; Odisha roads and Vietnam hospitals not yet loaded); shelter capacity, traffic,
      drainage, tide-locking of outfalls and wave run-up not modelled; wind averaging basis
      differences; hindsight in replays; loss ratio is an aggregate; media-reported damage figures
      for past storms are labelled and unverified.


B13. TENANTS AND SCALING
------------------------

     A tenant is a JSON file in tenants_data/: bounding box, focus point, languages, UTC offset
     and label, coast sectors (lat/lon, shelf width and depth, calibration k), recipient roles,
     replay storms, calibration event and attribution. Shipped: IN-AP (Visakhapatnam coast: 271
     hospitals, 88 substations, 3,110 road segments), IN-OR (Puri: 22 hospitals, 4 substations;
     road layer pending), VN (Hai Phong: 37 substations, 2,943 road segments; hospitals pending).
     Adding a region: copy a tenant file; run fetch_osm.py <ID> and prepare_tenant.py <ID>;
     optionally calibrate.py <ID>; restart. No code changes.
     Why this scales: the core model uses only global inputs (best tracks, open terrain, OSM, Earth
     Engine), so it is basin-agnostic (North Indian Ocean and West Pacific already run); the
     India-specific parts (IMD bulletins, LGD codes, CAP profile, IMD stage clock) live at the
     edges. Pilot pathway (proposal): week 1 shadow mode with one SDMA; weeks 2-3 a second state
     (file + language pack + data run); week 4 a CAP feed to a state EOC or the SACHET test
     channel via NDMA; later NCRMP Category II states and BRICS/APAC partners.


B14. DEPLOYMENT
---------------

     - One container, one URL: FastAPI serves the API and the UI, so there is no CORS setup and no
       API-URL build variable. Image: python:3.11-slim; uvicorn with ONE worker and threads (the
       engine is CPU-bound and holds per-tenant arrays in memory) and --proxy-headers so
       request.base_url reflects https behind Cloud Run's TLS termination (needed for the
       Telegram self-webhook URL).
     - Deploy from Cloud Shell with "gcloud run deploy --source ." (Cloud Build; no local Docker).
       Region asia-south1 (Mumbai) for latency to Indian users. Secrets (Gemini key, Telegram
       token) go in Secret Manager. Earth Engine uses the Cloud Run service account, whose project
       must be registered for EE. Full steps and troubleshooting: docs/DEPLOY.md.
     - --max-instances 1 keeps the in-memory simulation cache and the file-based audit chain
       consistent; production hardening (Firestore, retention-locked bucket, KMS) is on the roadmap.


B15. SECURITY AND RESPONSIBLE AI
--------------------------------

     - Secrets live only in .env (git-ignored) or Cloud Run secrets; a shared key should be rotated.
     - Human in the loop: the model can draft but never dispatch; dispatch is code behind a maker-
       checker state machine.
     - Untrusted text is data, not instructions; injected-instruction, URL and phone patterns are
       rejected by the validator; UI output is escaped.
     - CAP safety: always Exercise/Restricted, never an IMD/SDMA sender; checked before dispatch.
     - No personal data: tenant configs hold role placeholders, not real officials' contacts.
     - Transparency: every AI call, cache hit, fallback and validation result is visible; every
       advisory, CAP <note> and screen carries the "not an official warning" disclaimer.
     - Honest labelling everywhere: screening-level, in-sample calibration, simulated channels,
       illustrative finance figures.

B18. STATUS, KNOWN GAPS AND ROADMAP
-----------------------------------

  Working end to end on real data and tested (55 tests). Credentialed integrations degrade
  gracefully: Gemini (template / cached output), Earth Engine (open terrain), Telegram (simulated).
  Known gaps: Odisha roads and Vietnam hospitals/power layers are missing (Overpass timeouts);
  the AI cache needs priming when Gemini quota is available; the public deployment and demo video
  are pending at the time of writing.
  Roadmap: live forecast ingestion with IMD as the authority; shelf parameters from bathymetry and
  out-of-sample validation against Sentinel-1; blended NWP rainfall (GFS/ECMWF ensembles) and
  Google WeatherNext ensembles for the uncertainty cone; designated shelter registers and Health
  Facility Registry integration; Firestore state and a KMS-signed, bucket-anchored audit chain;
  SDMA integration through NDMA; Bhashini language adapters and voice bulletins; roof/material
  classification from photos with Gemini vision.

B20. GLOSSARY
-------------

  Storm surge / storm tide  Rise in sea level from a storm's low pressure and onshore wind / the
                            same plus the normal tide.
  Landfall / T0             When the storm centre reaches land / the event clock's zero, taken as the
                            closest approach to the region's focus point.
  Best track                An agency's after-the-fact analysis of a storm's position and intensity.
  IMD stages                Pre-Cyclone Watch (72 h), Cyclone Alert (48 h), Cyclone Warning (24 h),
                            Post-Landfall Outlook (12 h).
  DEM                       Digital elevation model: a grid of ground heights.
  Waterlogging basin        A closed low spot where rain water collects.
  Runoff corridor           A channel where rain water concentrates and flows.
  CAP                       Common Alerting Protocol, the international XML alert standard used by
                            NDMA's SACHET platform.
  SACHET / Web-DCRA         NDMA's CAP-based alert platform / IMD-NDMA's district hazard atlas.
  SDMA / DEOC / DDMA        State / District Emergency Operations Centre / District Disaster
                            Management Authority.
  Maker-checker             One person prepares, a different person approves.
  Anticipatory action       Releasing pre-agreed funds or actions when a forecast crosses a
                            threshold, before damage occurs.
  Basis risk                The chance a trigger fires with no loss, or misses a loss that occurs.
  Facts packet              The set of computed numbers an advisory is allowed to use.
  Tenant                    One region's configuration file.
  Circuit breaker           Logic that temporarily stops calling a failing service.
